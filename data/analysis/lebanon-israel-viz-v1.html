The words don’t cross the border

How often each country’s articles use each wording — Lebanon on the left, Israel on the right.

How is it described?

85% of Israeli articles use wording with no actor — “the pagers exploded”. Lebanese coverage is split down the middle.

What is it called?

In Israel it’s an “operation”. In Lebanon, “operation” barely exists — it’s a “massacre” or a “crime”.

Who are the dead?

The cleanest split in the data. Israel: “terrorists”, never “martyrs”. Lebanon: “martyrs”, almost never “terrorists”.

Whose information?

“According to foreign reports” — the formula for reporting what the military censor won’t confirm — exists only on the Israeli side.

Who says who did it?

You’d expect the attacker to boast and the victim to accuse. The data says the opposite: Israeli outlets mostly write “the pagers exploded” — no actor — a habit shaped by military censorship. Lebanese outlets name one more often. Each dot is one outlet, sized by article count. The extremes break ranks with their own side: Kikar (Israeli haredi) names an actor every single time; Hezbollah’s Al-Manar leads Lebanon.
