The ledger and the freeze

Forty-odd people died over two days. Whether anyone kept counting them depended on where you read the news.

Every casualty figure in this story comes from one man: Firas Abiad, Lebanon's health minister, who briefed from the ministry in Beirut as the numbers climbed. All four countries' media drew on his tallies. What they did with them diverged completely.

A running account, split by device, revised downward when the ministry cleaned up the count, closed with a surgical tally. In Lebanese copy Abiad is barely ever introduced. He appears as a ticker line ("Abiad: 25 martyrs and 608 wounded"), the way a wire quotes a stock price. He is named in only 4.5% of Lebanese records because he doesn't need naming; he is the house source.

Israel counted for 32 hours, then changed the metric

Sep 17, 14:28 UTC · Ynet

"~3,000 wounded, at least 9 killed: 'Hezbollah received the devices in recent days'"

Sep 18, 10:35 · Ynet

"Lebanon's health minister: 12 dead, including two children, in the device explosions"

Sep 18, 14:08 · Ynet

"New device attack in Lebanon: 14 dead and 450 wounded"

Sep 18, 20:50 · Ynet

"Lebanon: device-explosion death count rises to 20"

That 20:50 flash on September 18 is the last casualty-count headline in the Israeli corpus. The combined tolls Lebanon published over the following days, 32 and then 12 plus 27, never appear in an Israeli headline. Every Israeli number had come wrapped in attribution: "Lebanon's health ministry," "report." Counting was something Lebanon did, quoted at arm's length.

When a number finally returned, a week later, it measured something else:

Sep 25 · Ynet

"Report: ~1,500 Hezbollah terrorists blinded or lost a hand in the pager and device explosions"

דיווח: כ-1,500 מחבלי חיזבאללה התעוורו או איבדו יד בפיצוצי הביפרים ומכשירי הקשר

Sep 25 · Kikar HaShabbat

"A Hezbollah source: we lost 1,500 fighters in the pager attack"

The dead were a Lebanese statistic. The maimed were an Israeli result. Ynet's companion headline the same day makes the new metric explicit: 1,500 operatives "taken out of service."

Germany and the US outsourced the number

German and American desks ran the toll exactly twice. Day 0: Tagesschau's "Neun Tote und 2.750 Verletzte," USA Today's "8 dead and thousands more injured." Day 1's second wave: t-online's "20 Tote," ABC's "14 killed, 450 wounded." Then they stopped. The final split, 12 pager dead and 27 walkie-talkie dead, appears in exactly one German and one American record, in body text, never a headline. The next death tolls in German headlines belong to a different story, Spiegel's September 23 "Fast 500 Tote nach israelischen Angriffen." Neither country ever summed the pager dead.

What Germany and the US did instead was cite. Abiad is named in 14% of German and 14% of US records, three times his rate in Lebanon's own coverage, almost always in the same formula: "Lebanon's health minister, Firas Abiad, said…" The further from Beirut, the more the number needed a name attached. The man doing the counting became the citation that let a foreign desk print a figure and move on.

And nobody agreed anyway

In the second wave's first hours, ABC's AP live blog and Yahoo's Independent live blog covered the same event, in the same slot, with different tolls in the headline: "14 killed, 450 wounded" against "20 killed, 450 wounded." Ynet had both numbers six hours apart. No corpus ever reconciled the spread. Each country stopped at the number it had when it stopped looking.

Israel's attention followed the same curve as its counting: day 3 of the Israeli corpus holds four records, all supply-chain spy plot, zero casualty updates. The 1,500 figure came five days after that.
